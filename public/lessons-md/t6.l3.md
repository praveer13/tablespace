# T6.L3 — Recall Is a Curve, Not a Number

_Track T6: Vectors & HNSW · ~15 min · tablespace_

> ef_search sweeps, filtered ANN and iterative scans, pgvector vs purpose-built — and the capstone question: build, tune, or buy.
"Our index has 98% recall" is a sentence missing its denominator, its latency, and its WHERE clause. Recall is not a number. For any given build it is a **curve** of operating points swept over `ef_search` — recall traded against latency, continuously, at query time. Across builds it is a *family* of curves, one per (m, ef_construction), some strictly better than others. Everything in this lesson — honest measurement, the production cliff, the build-or-buy question — is an exercise in reading those curves like an engineer instead of a brochure.

---

## Measuring honestly

The protocol, in order:

1. **Ground truth first.** Compute the true top-k by brute force — exact scan, sort, limit. You already own this machine: it is your T5 executor running `ORDER BY vec <=> $1 LIMIT 10` over every row. Expensive, exact, and the only definition of "true" that exists.
2. **recall@k** = |your k ∩ true k| / k, averaged over a query set that looks like production — *including its filters*, which is the next section's whole point.
3. **Latency at the tail, not the middle.** Report p50 *and* p99. A p50 of 3ms with a p99 of 250ms is a different index than its average claims; T0's law applies verbatim — the slowest thing you do is the only thing your users remember. SLOs are written on tails.
4. **Sweep `ef_search`** from k upward; each value yields one (recall, latency) point. Connect them: that is this configuration's curve. Repeat per (m, ef_construction) build under consideration. Published benchmark curves — ann-benchmarks is the canonical gallery — are drawn on *someone else's corpus*: useful for shortlists, inadmissible as evidence about your data.
5. **Measure per workload, not globally.** A 96% average can be 99% for large tenants and 40% for small ones. The aggregate hides the cliff.

## The filtered-ANN cliff: INC-4

Now the failure this course named **INC-4 — The Recall Cliff**, because you will meet it in production or in Crash Week, whichever comes first. A RAG service on pgvector — HNSW, m=16, ef_search=40 — sails through staging. In production, every query carries `WHERE tenant_id = $1`, and tenants hold 0.5–2% of the rows each. Answer quality quietly collapses for small tenants: the retrieval step returns near-misses, or nothing. Nobody changed the index. Unfiltered recall@10 was 0.96 the whole time, glowing green on the dashboard.

The arithmetic is one line: the walk gathers roughly `ef_search` candidates *from the whole table*, and post-filtering keeps those passing the predicate. A 1% tenant's expected keepers out of 40 candidates is **0.4**. You asked for ten neighbors; the candidate pool does not contain one tenant row in expectation. And it is worse than the arithmetic, because the walk has no *reason* to wander into the tenant's subgraph — greedy routing follows distance, not your WHERE clause. Recall didn't degrade under the filter; **it was never measured under the filter.**

The fixes, in order of structural honesty:

- **Keep walking until k pass.** The index iterates: fetch candidates, filter, and if short, continue the walk from where it stopped. This is pgvector 0.8's **iterative scan** (`hnsw.iterative_scan`, strict or relaxed order, bounded by `hnsw.max_scan_tuples`, default 20,000). Same graph, honest stopping rule.
- **Filter inside the graph.** Apply the predicate *during* traversal, so the walk itself routes through passing nodes (the ACORN line of work — and what purpose-built vector databases bake in). Graph and filter stop being adversaries.
- **Partition when tenancy is hard.** A per-tenant index, or a table partition per tenant, makes the filter disappear into the catalog. Blunt, operationally real, sometimes exactly right.

Then re-measure **per tenant**, not globally. The incident's actual root cause was an aggregate metric.

---

> **[warning]** The tempting mitigation — crank ef_search until recall recovers — converts your index into a slow scan with extra steps. The walk still never enters the tenant's neighborhood (that is graph topology, not beam width), and you pay scan-class latency for candidates that still fail the filter: brute-force cost, reproduced inside the index path. Crash Week grades this option wrong. So does the p99 column.

---

## pgvector vs purpose-built: the honest bill

What does a dedicated vector database actually sell? Four things: **filtering woven into the index** (the previous section's hard fix, shipped as the default), **disk-native graphs** — DiskANN and its descendants keep a billion points on one node's SSDs instead of in RAM — **distribution** (sharding, replication) as a first-class feature, and **operations**: dashboards, tuning, upgrades, someone to call.

What you already hold with pgvector is the entire rest of this course: vectors are *rows*. They commit in the same transaction as the metadata they describe (T4), they are WAL-durable and crash-recovered with everything else (T3), they back up with the database, they join to the tables that own them. And since 0.8, iterative scans closed much of the filtering gap above.

So price the actual decision. At the capstone's scale — 5M × 1536-dim — the vectors are ~31GB and the graph ~1–2GB: **it fits in a 64GB instance's RAM with room for the buffer pool.** A purpose-built system buys you nothing at this size except a second system to run. At 500M vectors the vectors alone are ~3TB, RAM stops being a plan, and DiskANN-style SSD residency or a sharded service stops being a luxury. The curve tells you which world you are in — which is why the capstone grades the curve and not the opinion.

## The capstone question: build, tune, or buy

Every vector vendor hopes you answer with vibes. The capstone hands you the pieces of an honest answer instead: **5M embeddings, an SLO — recall@10 ≥ 0.95 at p99 < 50ms — and a price sheet.** Managed vector services price per million-vector-month; self-hosted pgvector prices as the instance big enough to hold 31GB of vectors plus graph plus working set; "build nothing" prices as the brute-force scan you can already run — your T5 executor plus an ORDER BY, latency courtesy of T0's arithmetic.

The lab-06 **curve** check *is* this computation in miniature: your measured QPS at your measured recall, on your seeded corpus, against the naive build's curve. From your own numbers you derive the only quantities the business case needs — nodes required to hold the SLO, cost per node, and the recall you would be buying or sacrificing at each price point. Your **planner_choice** check already practiced the micro-version: pick the index iff it genuinely wins. The capstone is the same sentence with a dollar sign. Bring your curve; leave the superstition.

---

- **0.4** — expected keepers, INC-4 (ef_search 40 × tenant share 1% = 0.4 candidates passing the filter, against k = 10 requested. The cliff, in one multiplication.)
- **0.96** — unfiltered recall@10, INC-4 (The dashboard stayed green the whole time. The metric was global; the failure was per-tenant.)
- **~31 GB** — the 5M-vector corpus (5M × 1536 dims × 4 bytes. Fits one 64GB node with the graph (~1–2GB) and buffer pool to spare.)
- **20,000** — hnsw.max_scan_tuples default (The bound on pgvector 0.8’s iterative scan: how far the walk may hunt for passing rows before giving up.)

**Q1. recall@10 is measured as…**
   A. the fraction of queries answered within the latency SLO
   B. 1 minus the index's average distance error versus brute force
   C. |your 10 results ∩ the brute-force true top-10| ÷ 10, averaged over the query set — truth defined by exact scan
   D. the share of the graph reachable from the entry point
   Answer: C — Recall has exactly one definition worth using: overlap with ground truth, where ground truth is the exact scan — your T5 executor as judge. Distance ratios and reachability are proxies, and proxies lie at exactly the edges you care about.

**Q2. INC-4: ef_search = 40, tenants at ~1% of rows, filtered recall collapsed while unfiltered stayed 0.96. Which mitigations address the mechanism? (Select all that apply.)**
   A. Iterative / index-aware scans: keep walking until k rows pass the filter (pgvector 0.8, bounded by max_scan_tuples)
   B. Rebuild the index nightly so the graph stays fresh
   C. In-graph filtering: apply the predicate during traversal, so the walk routes through passing nodes
   D. Raise ef_search to 4000 and eat the latency
   Answer: A, C — The mechanism is a candidate pool with ~0.4 expected passers and a walk with no reason to enter the tenant’s subgraph. Iterative scans change the stopping rule; in-graph filtering changes the walk. A rebuild changes nothing about the arithmetic, and ef_search = 4000 pays scan-class latency for candidates that still fail — Crash Week marks both wrong.

**Q3. Your build measures p50 = 3ms / p99 = 250ms at recall@10 = 0.99. The SLO is recall ≥ 0.95 at p99 < 50ms. Status, and the right move?**
   A. SLO met — recall has margin
   B. Violated on p99 — and the honest move is to down-sweep ef_search: trade the excess 4 points of recall for tail latency, sliding along the measured curve, then re-measure
   C. Violated on recall — 0.99 is below the 0.95 floor
   D. Violated on p50 — the middle is what users feel
   Answer: B — SLOs live on tails, and recall is a curve: you are holding recall you are not paid for and latency you cannot afford. Down-sweeping ef_search spends the surplus exactly where the SLO hurts. One measurement, one slide, one re-measurement — that is the whole operating loop.

---

The knob that fixed INC-4's class of bug: the **pgvector 0.8 release notes and README** — iterative index scans (`hnsw.iterative_scan`, `hnsw.max_scan_tuples`), the feature that taught Postgres to keep walking. The measurement culture: **ann-benchmarks.com** and its paper, **Aumüller, Bernhardsson, Faithfull, "ANN-Benchmarks: A Benchmarking Tool for Approximate Nearest Neighbor Algorithms" (Information Systems, 2020)** — recall-vs-QPS curves done right, and the reason "recall is a curve" is not an opinion. The SSD frontier: **Subramanya et al., "DiskANN" (NeurIPS 2019)** — a billion points on one node's NVMe, the architecture the purpose-built systems sell. The filtering frontier: **Patel et al., "ACORN" (SIGMOD 2024)** — predicate-agnostic filtered search inside the graph walk. And the textbook anchor for the whole track: **Petrov, Database Internals (O'Reilly, 2019)** — read its storage chapters with T6's eyes: everything the vectors live in is still pages, logs, and buffers. Then take your curve to Crash Week — INC-4 is waiting for someone who measured.
